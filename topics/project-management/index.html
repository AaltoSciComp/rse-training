Project management

This page gives some RSE-specific advice on project management. It doesn’t replace learning about standard project management practices, but is more targeted towards our usual projects. As wil

Being research does not mean that project management is easier. It means it’s harder, since understanding the vision and defining the scope must be woven together with understanding what is possible, each feeding off the other one. This is far harder than hardness(academic research) +hardness(normal projects) and the difficulty can sometimes increase unexpectedly.

  • Project processes: time-oriented project management guide.

  • Project pitfalls: what-goes-wrong oriented project management guide.

  • Project reporting: minimum mandatory reporting.

Why project management?

Think of any major enough human achievement [note: landing on the moon is a classic one, but that’s too chiché]. I’m usually less impressed with the technology of the achievement than the way so many people were able to work together to get it done. This is what project management achieves.

If a project is completely clear and routine to everyone from the start, collaboration can work without special management effort. The farther you are from that, the more special effort is needed - and the more structured it will tend to need to be. If it doesn’t exist, chaos will happen.

Almost anytime someone comes to us with (in their mind) a very difficult project, it hasn’t turned out to be that hard. The actually hard project seem easy but become hard because we aren’t clear on what to do, we can’t communicate well, there is no long term plan, it shifts over time, and so on. This is what project management can help with [research: it’s not making research any more predictable, but lets us react to the unpredictability.]

Terminology

This section presents various project management buzzwords, and it’s good if you are a bit suspicious about that. Still, they represent some main concepts which appear in many project management systems and are important for talking to each other about projects [note: and noticing how different people need information]. Don’t necessarily use these terms with customers (or even internally, and definitely don’t make separate documents with all of these names), but consider how they relate to each other and what info is needed when [small project: for many small projects or where we and the customer knows exactly what is needed - but that doesn’t mean you mentally make sure there is agreement on work].

Statement of Need

What the customer thinks they need.

Scope of Work

What we actually do, the “scope”. Should be developed after collaboration between customer and engineers.

Project vision

Not sure if others use this term, but I see it as a summary of statement of need + scope of work that tells what it’s about, so that we and customers can know why it’s important and generally what the outcomes would be. This can be used by customers and RSEs to make sure that the general plan is going OK.

Work breakdown structure

Taking the scope and breaking it down into specific tasks to do. Allows you to see dependencies and the schedule. For a dynamic research project, it may be impossible to know this in advance.

Project brief

OK, I am making up a definition here, but I compare this to the “project vision” in that it includes much more of the technical aspects. This is what a RSE could use to review what is going on and see if there expertise is needed to help with any of the design or work (e.g. data, web services, security, packaging, etc.).

Project manager

Manages the project. Heh. Realistically, many people in a project are doing many other things at once, or have only a limited view. The project manager makes sure they have an overall view and the various pieces aren’t being forgotten. This is the value for some of our projects [note: and can usually be done by one of the people in the project].

Iterative development

The idea that instead of planning out a whole project from the start, you plan in shorter chunks and adapt to what you see. Is basically standard in any case it can work. More specific names include “agile”, “scrum”, and many more. (The alternative is unthinkable in research.)

Figuring out the scope

Go slow in this phase. Multiple sessions of building up understanding is better than long emails with many questions.

There is a difference between the statement of need (what the customer wants) and scope of work (what we will actually do). Don’t confuse them or ask the customer to decide the scope of work for us (but also don’t do it without their input).

You may need to iterate many “bad” ideas to get good ones. This is why you should propose (multiple) reasonable-ish courses of action for discussion, and maybe it will improve from there. For a well-known field (for example civil engineering) the domain of “what can be done” is relatively well known. Not so for us.

And… how to figure out what to do? Surely you didn’t think this short page can teach you that. Finding out what to do is hard, there are many buzzwords like “design thinking”, “engineering thinking”, “radical creativity” [note: !!!] and more. Realize it’s a combination of science and art meeting technology and business [note: !!! (these are funny because they are Aalto University slogans)].

Iteration

We know about iteration from software development: agile, sprints, scrum, and so on. It’s only natural to us, and this should still be used. But at the research level, iteration can also be big phases of research, which is a research, not software development, consideration.

Example: data collection

I was working on a data collection project, and the broad vision was to get mobile phone data from people in a hospital environment. The whole project was implemented as phases, where each phase provides some useful result, even without the other phases.

  • Investigate different Android apps and run them on my own phone. Give some useful figures to be included in presentations about the power of data.

  • Run it on several group member’s phones as a test.

  • Run a student course and let the students use the tools. Students worked as researchers, understanding the data, with access only to their data, and their final report was about what the data told them. This helped to guide future research.

  • Yet another student course.

  • Pilot with some people in a hospital.

  • Full study with some people in a hospital.

The phases were not just useful for the technical development, but also helping the relevant ethics/legal staff to get used to our work before we got to more demanding things. The project could not have been done skipping many of the middle steps, and overall it took years - yet each phase was designed to provide some benefit. This is kind of an extreme example, but it’s representative of how to design things. It requires engineering-application cooperation to figure this out.

Customers will often only have some end goal in mind, and often think that requesting the end goal will work. We need to tell them the risks of going straight to the end goal, and then also help to come up with the right phases that are useful to them. This requires knowing a lot about their work - maybe as far as shadowing them some.

This can also be simpler, for example “before we can plan turning your software into a web server, we have to figure out your script. We will first do that before even planning out the web server part.”

Creating the project phases is usually one of the hardest part of planning.

The project brief

When working on projects, we often need to describe the project to others. This can be summarizing the actions to the customer, or summarizing the project to other internal people for their own specific feedback (ethics, security, web dev, etc.) [note: For a project to be successful, you need to be able to efficiently use the knowledge of these experts, given their limited time. Don’t make them dig to figure out what the story is]. This quick summary is the project brief. By having one description (which is at a good level of detail), we can more easily take advantage of all of our skills. (See also Communications)

This is not the current status report. This is somehow a summary of the customer’s needs, scope of work, expected challenges and what happens for them. It can be updated as the project develops, but (again) isn’t simply the current status. Think of it this way: If a random RSE reads the project brief, are they able to tell if they have any important information or skills they should offer to the project? [note: For example, “we have tried that before and it wasn’t a good idea”, “I think this needs ethics review”, etc.]

Present options to the customer

It can be overwhelming to try to figure out the right path - especially when there is no one perfect path. It’s hard to tell a customer the whole range of possibilities and help them make a meaningful decision (unless they are already very good at the topic).

To simplify, can you narrow things down into some representative options, for example 3-4, and present these along with the trade-offs. Then, you can use these to discuss the various trade-offs and they can give meaningful guidance in what the right direction is. Of course, this may not immediately lead you to the plan, but can help you iterate further.
