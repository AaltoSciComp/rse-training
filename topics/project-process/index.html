Project processes

This page describes in general our project flow, from outreach to completion, and what should be considered at each stage. It is probably not that specific to Aalto RSE. This should not be taken as some exact requirements for every project. It’s a list of how to avoid common traps, and if you understand it and adjust to each particular project you have, you will be good.

See also

  • For the minimum mandatory reporting work, see Project reporting. That tells the minimum you have to do to actually get “permission” to work on a project and track it, this is general advice.

  • For the user-focused view of how projects work, see Project lifecycle

  • For an old version of this page, see OLD Project process.

Some common project-specific changes: If the work is clear from the start, you often begin the work immediately with less planning [risks: customers disappearing, no clear end, goals weren’t actually as clear as you thought]. You commonly work for a research group and know a lot of the background already [risks: people are over-committed and the customers don’t maintain interest, the right RSE expertise is not used, or the customers don’t assemble the right time and team to support it].

Recall that a “project” for our team is something big enough to need tracking and planning (going beyond the question-answer of garage-type support). (Don’t forget that, confusingly, sometimes people say “project” for anything they do, even if not tracked as a project).

Why a project process?

We are many people working together, and different people are involved in projects at different times. It is good to be able to convey to each other what stage something is at, and what types of help may be needed at different times.

It also helps us to remember things at each step.

However, it can be adjusted and changed depending on what each project needs, and shouldn’t be blindly applied. (though appearing structured gives a good impression to customers, even if not strictly needed.)

Summary

Summary

  • There is a funnel from everyone we talk to, to those who actively talk about a project, to those doing a project. Help people down that funnel by the actions on this page.

  • Research is not like typical consulting projects in that the outcome (or even achievability) is not as likely to be well-known in advance.

  • Our customers tend to have less experience doing projects and working with us, so we need to take more care to manage them and help them through their work

Outreach

Summary

  • Tell customers about what we do and help them understand how we can help. Check Outreach material for ideas.

  • You can give basic ideas, brainstorm, and think of ideas even before there is a “project”. Remember it may be difficult for them to understand what we can do.

  • Invite them to talk with us for more, but also…

  • Take their contact info and follow up with them if appropriate [note: for example, after a few weeks so they can think about it some - maybe even asking someone else who has different knowledge to come with you or do it].

We are often out talking to researchers to tell them about what we do. This is just the first introduction, so there is not yet a concrete project or anything to do, but if someone may have something, one should try to work on developing the idea.

You should:

  • Talk and bounce initial ideas off of them.

(depending on the circumstances,) Make a note of their contact info, and either you or someone else follow up. In the follow up you could:

  • Ask how things are going

  • Ask if they would like to meet to talk further (could be as simple as lunch or chatting in the coffee room)

Unless we are fully booked, try not to leave good potential customers with only a “come to garage” - yes, tell them to come to garage, but also do some active follow-up.

Once someone comes with something concrete enough to work on, we go to the proper project process below.

Kick-off meeting (statement of need)

Summary

  • Get all information needed to make the plan.

  • Make sure everyone is on the same page (even those not taking part in detailed planning or expectation)

  • Figure out the customer’s problem [note: not only focusing on a proposed solution] and what it means to be done, even if it will be adjusted later.

  • In the common case where the long-term plan cannot be know, plan for iterations.

  • You can use the project template document

The kick-off meeting is the first real project step (though it’s not guaranteed to become a project from here). This is the time to determine whether a project is viable by understanding what is needed. Make sure the customer’s supervisor is on board, if relevant [note: Not relevant for small, “phd-projects”. More relevant when it is large, purpose is less clear, many people are involved, or it requires funding]. In all but the simplest cases, it is recommended to have people involved - at least one of whom is a certified [note: there is no certification process, so take this as humor] project planner.

One main goal of this meeting is to figure out what the customer wants (“statement of need”), as in, what is desired to be included or not [scope: you could call this the scope, but realize the customer may not know what they need yet, so it’s hard to say it is the scope. Other engineers separate the Statement of Need (what customers think they need) from the Scope of Work (engineer-designed proposal for what to do).]. One way to think is “what does it mean to be done?”

The customers usually have some vision of their needs, not necessarily the way of getting there or what exactly should be included. So, one needs to work with them to figure out some initial ideas and what the scope could be.

The main goals are to:

  • Get all the information you need (no surprises later).

  • Understand what the project needs (or at least, what the customers think they need), “what does it mean to be done”, so that you can start making proposals.

  • Discuss roles, responsibilities, and expectations.

  • Establish communication channels and reporting mechanisms.

  • Have at least an initial discussion about the risks.

  • Tell the customer what to expect next.

  • Non-goal: make commitments before people know what they are committing to.

Technical planning

Summary

  • Take the time for background research to find the right plan (look at their current work, other background research) and call in other experts to give comments.

  • Don’t necessarily immediately decide and develop one plan. There are usually trade-offs, prepare several alternatives and discuss with the customer - with your recommendation [note: This also allows the customer to feel engaged.].

  • Planning can be iterative (different phases with work in between).

  • Ensure an OK from everyone before going on.

The technical planning phase involves detailed planning of the technical aspects of the project. In short, the customer has a broad idea (Statement of Need). Now you try to refine it into the actual work to do.

You may not be able to decide one exact path yourself, and you probably shouldn’t try too hard at this either. Instead, understand the problem and see if you can develop several reasonable representative solutions that you can present to the customer for decision. You are the expert so you can make a recommendation: presenting the options helps to keep the customer engaged, feeling like they are a part of it, and also helps them to understand what kind of trade-offs there are.

This may, or may not, be obvious from the start. It may, or may not, be done at the same time as the kick-off. It may or may not require a lot of background work before a plan is made. It may require multiple experts in different aspects brainstorming and giving ideas, and you need to track these people down and ask them. [the project brief: in this case, you’ll be giving a summary of the project to many different people. You need to prepare the description carefully so they can understand quickly and figure out what they can contribute. Practice this and preferably write it down - concisely.]

Being research, it may or may not be possible to make a real plan (who knows what actually can, or can’t, work). Don’t over-plan. The plan could be more about how the unknowns are approached, rather than exactly what will happen. The uncertainty should be presented back to the customers as part of the plan.

Projects should be planned so that no one is alone. If someone might need mentoring, it should be there. If someone should be around to teach new skills during the project, this should be planned.

The main goals are to:

  • Go from the customer’s need to (several possible) solutions.

  • Present these back to the customer for feedback and decision.

  • Identify further potential risks and mitigation strategies.

  • Plan the technical tasks and ensure there are enough resources to do it.

Go or no-go decision

Summary

  • Don’t forget to present all information to make a formal-enough decision to do the project or not.

  • Should we do it? as much as Can we do it?.

  • It is OK to say “no”, suggest others to do the project, instead of us, or suggest other solutions.

  • Re-iterate expectations, who does what, and risks.

  • Make sure things are written down (somewhere) for future reference.

A decision to do a project can only really be made after everything is known (both on our side and the customer’s side). Until that point, it’s more like garage support (even if we have a project issue about it). There should be something written, in proportion to how demanding the project is.

The choice to begin must equally be made by us (the research engineers). We should critically analyze the project and say “no” if needed. At least think “is the work understood?”, “are the expectations well-defined?” [note: “we really don’t know if this is possible” is a reasonable expectation, too, as long as everyone is on board], “what are the risks?”, and “can we communicate well enough?”

As a side note here: when the future can’t be predicted (as in, there are actual uncertainties that can’t be planned out), don’t try to force it. Plan a phased project with different intermediate objectives and go/no-go decisions.

With a go, time should be reserved (time planning spreadsheet).

Work during the project

Summary

  • Communicate well. It’s our job.

  • Being research, the end goal may have been uncertain at the start and is likely changing over time. Keep active communication going.

  • You must keep the customers well-informed and part of the loop.

  • If the goals change or you get to a new phase, go back to planning.

  • If customers ever disappear, ask for help rather than continue working on the wrong thing.

Effective communication and collaboration are crucial throughout the project. Customers shouldn’t expect that RSEs disappear and return with a final product, communication has to be planned from the start. Ideally, move to use whatever channels the customer prefers.

If nothing else, a message to customers each week about the status shows we are keeping things up to date. The trail of messages implies professionalism and in case of questions, shows we were doing our part. This is the minimum; ideally there are much more frequent customer meetings to actively test and comment on your work.

Sometimes projects drift [note: Time commitment, expectations, definition of done, risks, timeline, outcome, etc.], and this can also be a bit dangerous, because you go into unplanned-mode. If this is expected (and communicated well), it can be OK, but sometimes you will need to stop and go back to planning mode. It’s probably much faster now.

You shouldn’t be alone - ask for help when needed, even broad discussions about what you are doing. Part of our culture is helping each other first, but you need to make the opportunities (your team leaders and mentors can only go so far to make this happen).

The main goals are to:

  • Do high-quality work

  • Communicate and keep all stakeholders informed of progress and changes.

  • Facilitate collaboration among team members.

  • Address issues and risks promptly.

  • Ensure that project documentation is up-to-date.

Wrap-up

Summary

  • Being research, projects have a way of going on forever. Try to have some end of your active phase (may need to be planned in advance).

  • Take the time to reflect some and make the final reports.

  • It doesn’t mean you stop all communication with the customers.

The wrap-up phase occurs at the end of the project. If you look at our issue tracker, you can probably see many ancient projects that haven’t been wrapped up (and maybe not even started). This isn’t good for our tracking, and also not good since customers may not be getting any closure.

From the initial planning, think “what is done?” so you can try to get there. If there is no done, there may be an ongoing need. That is fine, customers can hire us “on retainer” for miscellaneous long-term tasks.

The main goals are to:

  • Get to some point where you can stop thinking about the project.

  • Ensure the customer has gotten what they want out of it.

  • Use the reporting procedures to make sure our work is documented and the impact will appear in our yearly report.

  • Reflect lessons learned and best practices (present in RSE meeting and/or write down).

  • Report time and resource usage, and ask about /timesaved for the project.

Maintenance and long-term support

Summary

  • Research goes on and projects have a way of blending into each other. This means we are often called to do ongoing maintenance of finished work.

  • You can say the policy is “Once funding or planned time runs out, we can’t promise time, but we will best-effort help you via garage-level support.”

  • If ongoing maintenance is likely, discuss it from the start.

  • Avoid doing a project where the customers are not able to manage anything without you (unless that’s the explicit goal) [note: In other words, don’t create something so complex that only you or an expert RSE can understand it. Make it suitable to the users.]

The maintenance and support phase occurs after the project has been completed and delivered. One of our advantages is that we don’t disappear like a commercial consultant would, but this has to be balanced with “projects can go on forever” from above.

Discuss long-term maintenance from the earliest phases. Are you designing something which can’t easily be maintained by the customers? Is there a chance that, once they see it, they can learn how to maintain (+ use + update) themselves? Do you need to budget a long-term retainer to keep their work viable? These are all hard questions that researchers aren’t used to thinking about.

Most important from our side: we should avoid getting so burdened with unfunded old work, that we don’t have time to do any new work. (If the work has ongoing funding for maintenance, then it’s current and it’s OK to maintain long-term.)

The ongoing maintenance could include anything from:

  • Provide support so that the customers can do maintenance on their own.

  • Provide active maintenance for things that go wrong (bugs, necessary upgrades).

  • Do minor new development as needed to support new things.

  • Scientific support for publication or new grants.

Dropped projects

Summary

  • Realize when projects are dropped without a completion.

  • We should communicate with customers so it’s clear what expectations are.

Sometimes, projects may be discontinued or paused before completion - sometimes even without being announced (the customers just stop responding actively). This often happens in research, but when we are involved, it needs to be communicated well. The “mostly dropped but someone thinks it is still going on” stage is especially dangerous, due to mis-matched expectations.

When projects are not funded (or more precisely, funded by someone else or basic funding), the one dropping the project doesn’t lose anything. This makes it especially risky for us, because we spend someone’s money but can’t show results. Part of this can be justified as supporting research, but it’s worth discussing with the customer.

If the project is discontinued or paused, it is important to properly document the reasons and any work completed:

  • Make it clear that we won’t continue working on a project.

  • Document any work completed and lessons learned.

  • Reallocate planned time to other projects as needed.
