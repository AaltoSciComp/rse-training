Project processes

This page describes in general what to consider when working on projects. It is probably not that specific to Aalto RSE, and probably needs adjustment to any individual project. Still, it is useful to have one place with good practices and a description of all the way that things can go wrong.

See also

  • For the minimum mandatory reporting work, see Project reporting.

  • For the user-focused view, see Project lifecycle

  • For an old version of this page that should be copied here, see OLD Project process.

Terminology:

Project

Anything large enough to need tracking and structure. Has an issue in RSE project tracker. Rule of thumb for the size is anything that requires more than a couple of hours of work.

Garage support

Any small things we do that don’t require extensive tracking on our side. Something where we mainly answer requests and is pushed forward by the customer coming to talk to us. May or may not actually come through garage.

Project (informal)

Confusingly, can sometimes mean both “project” and “garage support”.

Why project management?

If a project is completely clear to everyone from the start, collaboration without special management effort can work. The farther things come from “routine”, the more is needed - and the more structured it will tend to need to be. If it doesn’t exist, beware of chaos.

  • Make sure that we and our customers have the same goals.

  • Define clear goals, objectives, deliverables, and deadlines.

  • Keep track of things long-term.

  • Record where our time goes, so that we can get good reports for our funders, so that we will continue to be well-funded in the future.

  • Avoid projects which we may not be successful at due to {undefined goals, impossible goals, or generally not knowing what is going on}.

  • Prevent over-committing ourselves.

  • Show that our output work roughly corresponds to input funding (by unit, and goals of those units).

  • Provide sound financial tracking for projects that need it.

Summary

Summary

  • There is a funnel from everyone we talk to, to those who actively talk about a project, to those doing a project. Help people down that funnel by the actions on this page.

  • Research is not like typical consulting projects in that the outcome (or even achievable) is not as likely to be well-known in advance.

  • Our customers tend to have less experience doing projects and working with us, so we need to take more care to manage them and help them through their work

Outreach

Summary

  • Tell customers about what we do and help them understand how we can help. Check Outreach material for ideas.

  • You can give basic ideas, brainstorm, and think of ideas even before there is a “project”. Remember it may be difficult for them to understand what we can do.

  • Invite them to talk with us for more, but also…

  • Take their contact info and follow up with them if appropriate [note: for example after a few weeks so they can think about it some - maybe even asking someone else who has different knowledge to do come with you or do it].

We are often out talking to researchers to tell them about what we do. This is just the first introduction, so there is not yet a concrete project or anything to do, but if someone may have something, one should try to work on developing the idea.

You should:

  • Talk and bounce initial ideas off of them.

(depending on the circumstances,) Make a note of their contact info, and either you or someone else follow up. In the follow up you could:

  • Ask how things are going

  • Ask if they would like to meet to talk further (could be as simple as lunch or chatting in the coffee room)

Unless we are fully booked, try not to leave good potential customer with only a “come to garage” - yes, leave them with that, but also do some follow-up.

Kick-off meeting (scope)

Summary

  • Get all information needed to make the plan.

  • Make sure everyone is on the same page (even those not taking part in detailed planning or expectation)

  • Figure out the initial desired scope, even if it will be adjusted later.

  • In the common case where the long-term plan can not be know, plan for iterations.

The kick-off meeting is the first real project step (though, it’s not guaranteed to become a project from here). This is the time to determine the overall viability of a project or not by understanding what is needed. Make sure the customer’s supervisor is on board, if relevant [note: Not relevant for small, “phd-projects”. More relevant when it is large, purpose is less clear, many people are involved, or it requires funding]. In all but the simplest cases, it is recommended to have the team leader or a senior RSE involved in this meeting as well.

One main goal of this meeting is to figure out the scope, as in, what is desired to be included or not. The customers usually has some vision of their needs, not necessarily the way of getting there or what exactly should be included. So, one needs to work with them to figure out some initial ideas and what the scope could be.

The main goals are to:

  • Get all the information you need early (no surprises later)

  • Introduce the project team and stakeholders.

  • Understand the project objectives, scope, and deliverables.

  • Discuss roles, responsibilities, and expectations.

  • Establish communication channels and reporting mechanisms.

  • Confirm the project timeline and key milestones.

This meeting ensures that everyone is aligned and ready to begin the project work.

Technical planning

Summary

  • Take the time to go from the needs and general plan identified above to a specific, implantable plan.

  • This may require more help than just the RSEs in the previous meeting.

  • This stage can be iterative and customers should be a part of it as much as needed.

  • Ensure an OK from everyone before going on.

The technical planning phase involves detailed planning of the technical aspects of the project. This may, or may not, be at the same time as the kick-off (or it may start there and carry on later).

The main goals are to:

  • Define the technical requirements and specifications.

  • Identify potential risks and mitigation strategies.

  • Plan the technical tasks and allocate resources.

  • Establish development and testing procedures.

Without a good plan, work can become disorganized. Taking the time to plan thoroughly is crucial for the success of the project. Consulting with team members and stakeholders is important to ensure that all perspectives are considered. Taking the time to discuss the plan with the customer and team leader helps ensure that the plan is realistic and aligned with expectations.

This phase ensures that the project has a clear technical roadmap and that potential issues are addressed early. Listing the technical tasks and technologies and their dependencies helps in tracking progress and managing resources effectively.

Each project will have two RSEs assigned to it, responsible for different aspects of the technical work. The work distribution is not necessarily equal and should be based on expertise and project needs.

Communication and work during the project

Summary

  • Being research, the end goal was uncertain at the start and likely is changing over time.

  • You must keep the customers will-informed and part of the loop.

  • If customers ever disappear, ask advice rather than continue working on the wrong thing.

Effective communication and collaboration are crucial throughout the project. Using customer’s preferred communication channel is highly recommended; as the primary stakeholders should be kept happy and informed through regular updates. Regular meetings are also recommended, and using status reports and collaborative tools are essential to achieve these goals.

To make sure you get help when needed, and identify issues in early stages, it is important to discuss the project with your team and team leader too.

The main goals are to:

  • Keep all stakeholders informed of progress and changes.

  • Facilitate collaboration among team members.

  • Address issues and risks promptly.

  • Ensure that project documentation is up-to-date.

Regular meetings, status reports, and collaborative tools help maintain alignment and transparency during the project.

Wrap-up

Summary

  • Being research, projects have a way of going on forever. Try to have some end of your active phase (may need to be planned in advance).

  • Take the time to get some reflect and get statistics.

  • It doesn’t mean you stop all communication.

The wrap-up phase occurs at the end of the project.

The main goals are to:

  • Review project outcomes and deliverables.

  • Assess project performance against objectives.

  • Document lessons learned and best practices.

  • Report time and resource usage, and ask about /timesaved for the project.

This phase ensures that the project is formally closed and that valuable insights are captured for future projects.

Maintenance and support

Summary

  • Research goes on and projects blend in to each other. You do not have to completely stop support at the end.

  • If ongoing maintenance is likely, discuss it from the start.

  • Avoid doing a project where the customers are not able to manage anything without you (unless that’s the explicit goal) [note: In other words, don’t create something so complex that only you or an expert RSE can understand it. Make it suitable to the users.]

The maintenance and support phase occurs after the project has been completed and delivered.

The main goals are to:

  • Address any issues or bugs that arise post-deployment.

  • Provide ongoing support to users and stakeholders.

  • Implement updates and improvements as needed.

  • Ensure the continued performance and reliability of the project.

This phase ensures that the project remains functional and valuable over time, and that any emerging needs are addressed.

Dropped projects

Sometimes, projects may be discontinued or paused before completion. As we are working with researchers, their focus may shift to another project or area of research, leading to the discontinuation or pausing of the current project. If the project is discontinued or paused, it is important to properly document the reasons and any work completed:

  • Document any work completed and lessons learned.

  • Reallocate resources to other projects as needed.

  • Communicate the decision to all stakeholders and team leader

This phase ensures that even discontinued projects are properly closed and that valuable insights are captured for future initiatives.
