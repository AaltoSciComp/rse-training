Project pitfalls
================

This is a list of common pitfalls we have seen when running customer
projects.  It's designed to help us learn from the past, so we don't
repeat it.  By its nature, this is what goes wrong, not what you can
do.  See other pages for what you should actually do.

Things on this page should not be identifiable to projects.  In fact,
each item on the page should an invented mix of multiple projects, so that
there is even less chance of any one point identifying something
:inote:`machine-generated text is good at hallucinating new project
examples for known pitfalls`.



Who is waiting for who?
-----------------------

The customer thinks they are waiting for us to do something, but we
think we are waiting for the customer to do something.  This can
easily happen when someone asks a question and it isn't noticed, but
can also happen at a larger scale ("I didn't know we made the final
decision that we wanted this.")

*We should make sure we know who is the "prime mover" at all times, to
know who will drive things forward.*



"AI"-generated design or starting example
-----------------------------------------

Customers come with a project idea, but it is machine generated.
Thus, it may not be fully thought out, thus discussions about the
project go in circles.  Similarly, they may have some initial code,
but it's mostly machine generated and thus isn't a good starting
point.

*We should recognize and ask about these situations, and know we need
to take step back before diving in.  It may very well mean we need to
spend a lot of time on initial understanding, before thinking about
what they are actually asking us to do.*



Not getting frequent enough feedback
------------------------------------

We work on a project for too long without sharing the progress with
the customers.  When they do see, it wasn't quite what they expected.

*This is common for many types of projects.  There needs to be
proactive showing the customer what is happening, making demos,
getting it into their hands.  If customers don't have time to follow
our progress, this is a major red flag.*



Doing what the customer asks
----------------------------

Sometimes, the customer needs X, thinks Y can do it, so asks for Y.
In reality Y wasn't the best way to get X done, and we waste lots of
time (this is the definition of the `XY problem
<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/XY_problem>`__.  In research, this can
be especially common, since the problem is often under development as
much as the solution.

*Spend an appropriate amount of time understanding the big picture of
the customer's ultimate need, not just what they ask for.  If they
don't have time for this, consider if we should do the project.  For
the biggest projects, a few online meetings isn't enough.  Go visit
them, join their group meetings, etc.*



Focusing only on the technical task
-----------------------------------

Customers come and we focus on what they ask.  However, this fits into
a bigger research group, with different people with different skills.
"Who can do what?", "How do people feel about computers?", "How do
people relate to the work?", "Who is able to use and maintain the
result?", and so on, are affect the technical outcome.

*Like above, one needs to spend enough time getting to know your
customers to make sure that the technological solution is appropriate
to them.*



Goal drift
----------

A project starts off with one idea, which may or may not be
accomplished, but the goal drifts over time.  The project goes on in a
reactive mode, possibly without a direction or defined end point.  The
new goals may not be possible, a good idea, or have enough staff to
accomplish.  We may be acting more like researchers, investigating
random things, hoping something comes out.

*We* are *researchers and if the project goal involves testing out
various things and we are paid to do so, this isn't necessarily bad.
Still, it good to identify these places and make sure our actions are
aligned with everyone's expectations, and there are no "planning
pitfalls" surfacing.*



Taking forever analyzing options
--------------------------------

Often times, the best solution isn't clear.  We like analyzing things,
and like getting things right.  We think forever about things,
essentially going in circles re-analyzing the options.  Everything is
a trade-off and some decision needs to be made.

*I think it's worth recognizing these cases, and there is no perfect
answer.  Consider: who should make the decision about the trade-offs?
Then, can you summarize the trade-offs (in writing) clearly enough
that everyone can understand, comment, and then support the decider in
making the choice?  This hopefully limits the time taken to clarify
options, discussion/understand, then making the least-bad decision.*



Inconsistent names of projects
------------------------------

Sometimes we and/or customers don't invent a consistent,
representative name of a project.  Then, we get confused about what
project we are talking about.  Gitlab issue numbers help, but we don't
use them in daily conversation.

*It's worth asking the name early on and making sure it is
representative and distinct (and the first three words are unique and
representative, too).*
